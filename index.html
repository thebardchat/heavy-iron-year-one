The Legal Foundation — Read This First

Renting means a customer takes possession of your machine and pays you for access to it. Providing a service means you (or your employee) operate the machine and the customer pays for the outcome — the hole dug, the debris cleared, the operator trained. These are legally distinct in every state. The three proposals below operate entirely in the service model. The restriction never becomes an issue.

Site Services & Excavation Company

Buy the machine. Operate it yourself or hire an operator. Bill jobs by the hour or by the project. The customer pays for work done — never for the machine itself. Nothing about this touches a rental restriction.

What You Actually Do

Land clearing · Site preparation · Utility trenching · Pond digging · Demolition · Drainage work · Driveway building · Foundation excavation · Debris removal · Road grading

An excavator is the single most versatile piece of iron on a jobsite. With the right attachments — a bucket swap, a thumb, a hydraulic breaker — one machine does all of the above. Your service rate bundles the machine, fuel, transportation, and your operator into one line item. The customer has no idea what the machine cost you. They know what the hole costs them.

Heavy Equipment Operator Training Academy

There are more than 400,000 unfilled equipment operator positions in the US right now. Schools that train operators are booked out. The equipment is your classroom. Tuition, not rent, is the revenue model.

Why This Works

You are not renting the excavator to students. You are providing a credentialed educational service that uses the equipment as an instructional tool — the same way a welding school uses welders and a culinary school uses ovens. This is legal under every state's framework. Tuition revenue is the income. No restriction applies.
Industry tuition benchmarks (2026): Earthmover School (FL) — $3,900–$7,500 per course. Performance Training Solutions — $4,500–$8,000 depending on specialty. VocationalTrainingHQ average — $5,200 per student for a 2-week program. These are not made up — they are what people pay right now.

Material Extraction & Sales

You don't sell or rent the machine. You sell what the machine produces. Fill dirt, topsoil, gravel, and aggregate are commodities with real market pricing — and there is constant demand from developers, landscapers, and contractors who need material hauled in or taken out.

How It Works

Two-sided revenue: Contractors and homeowners pay you to haul away excess dirt and debris from their sites. Simultaneously, developers and landscapers pay you to deliver fill material to their sites. You're making money on both ends of the same cubic yard. This is the dirt broker model, and it's been around forever for good reason.

Government Bidding Jobs

When any government body — city, county, state, or federal — needs construction work done, they are legally required to open it to competitive bids. That means any licensed, bonded contractor can win the contract. Government pays more, pays reliably, and has an endless supply of work.

This Is a Revenue Multiplier, Not a Standalone Model

Government bidding stacks on top of whichever proposal you choose. You don't stop doing private work — you add government contracts to fill gaps in your schedule and command a higher rate. Think of it as upgrading 20–40% of your annual hours from $200/hr private jobs to $250–280/hr government-rate jobs.

The Three Tiers — Start Here, Not There

Roads · Drainage · Parks · Public Utilities · Demolition
This is where you start. City and county contracts are smaller (often $10K–$100K), have the lowest bonding requirements, pay the fastest (30–60 day net), and have the least paperwork. Many municipalities have no bonding requirement at all under $50,000. You can register and bid within 30 days of forming your LLC. Search your county's procurement portal — most post bids publicly. This is real money that most small contractors ignore because they think government work is too complicated.

How to Start — The 30-Day Checklist

Week 1: Form LLC, get EIN from IRS.gov (free, instant online), open business checking account.

Week 2: Register on SAM.gov (free — takes 7–14 days to activate). Bookmark your county's procurement / purchasing portal. Search for active bids in excavation, site work, drainage, and demolition.

Week 3: Get a Certificate of Insurance naming the relevant government entity as additionally insured (your insurance agent does this in 24 hours). Get a business license from your city/county.

Week 4: Submit your first bid. Start with a county road repair or drainage ditch job — scope is simple, competition is manageable. You will not win your first bid. That is fine. The act of bidding teaches you the format, the pricing, and the market faster than anything else.

Verdict: Low Risk, Meaningful Upside — Do It Alongside Everything Else

Even one or two county contracts in Year One builds the past-performance record that unlocks bigger bids in Year Two and Year Three. The Veteran Edge — An Unfair Advantage (Legally)

The below-market acquisition theory is no longer a theory. It has a name, a legal basis, and a real (if recently relocated) application process behind it. This tab explains what it is, what it unlocks, and why it compounds into something no non-veteran competitor can touch.

⚠ CORRECTED 2026-09-01 — Portal Moved, Price Mechanism Was Wrong

GSAxcess.gov was decommissioned July 24, 2023 — confirmed dead (the domain no longer resolves). The replacement is PPMS.gov, but you don't apply there directly: you register through your State Agency for Surplus Property (ADECA for Alabama, adeca.alabama.gov/surplus/) after VetCert is approved. The price is also not "15–20% of replacement cost" — it's a service charge based on the government's original acquisition cost (what they paid when new), roughly mid-single-digits to ~15% depending on state and property type, plus shipping. Every specific dollar figure on this page ($34,500, $92,000, etc.) is the old illustrative estimate, not a verified real transaction — get a real ADECA quote before committing capital. Sourced live by Sapper (this project's Grok-based intelligence bot); full detail in CLAUDE.md's Acquisition Program Correction section.

✓ VERIFIED: The Below-Market Acquisition Path Is Real. It's the VOSB Surplus Property Program.

Every year, federal agencies — the military, the Army Corps of Engineers, the Forest Service, the Department of Transportation — retire equipment from their fleets. Rather than auction it publicly, they must first offer it to priority groups. Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned businesses are at the top of that priority list, backed by Public Law 115-416 (Veterans Small Business Enhancement Act of 2018), codified at 13 CFR Part 128 Subpart G. Apply through your state's Surplus Property Agency (ADECA in Alabama) after VetCert approval; browse/request via PPMS.gov. The price is a service charge on the government's original acquisition cost — not simply a percentage of today's replacement value.

What Flows Through the Program

  • ⚙ Excavators, dozers, graders, loaders from military base fleet rotations

SDVOSB Status — What It Actually Unlocks

SDVOSB stands for Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business. With 51%+ ownership and a service-connected disability rating, this designation is registered through the VA's Veteran Small Business Certification (VetCert) program at veteransbusinesscertification.va.gov and then SAM.gov. Once active, it opens four separate competitive advantages simultaneously.

① Surplus Property Priority Access

Acquire heavy equipment at a service-charge price well under replacement cost before the general public ever sees it. This is the engine of the whole model.

② Federal Set-Aside Contracts

Certain federal contracts are reserved exclusively for SDVOSBs. No non-veteran competitor can bid. Sole-source awards go up to $4.5M for services, no competition required.

③ VA Veterans First Program

The VA is legally required to contract with SDVOSBs before considering any other source. Site clearing, infrastructure maintenance, campus construction — all eligible.

④ P&T Tax Advantages

100% Permanent & Total (P&T) rating: VA disability comp is federal income tax free. Alabama fully exempts P&T veterans from property tax. Combined with Section 179 on the business side, the effective cost basis is dramatically lower.

Operating Agreement — The Non-Negotiables

Whatever structure they choose, these provisions must be in the agreement. These are the things that protect the friendship when life happens — and life always happens.

Decision Authority

Any spend over $10,000 requires both partners to sign off. Day-to-day operations: Shane calls it. Surplus-property acquisitions and federal contracts: Josh calls it. Major direction changes: unanimous.

Buyout Clause

If either partner wants out, the other has right of first refusal at fair market value — determined by a neutral third-party appraiser. No forcing a sale, no surprise exits. This is what saves friendships when life changes direction.

SDVOSB Protection

Neither partner can transfer, dilute, or encumber Josh's 51%+ ownership stake without a full partnership agreement amendment. This clause cannot be waived. The SDVOSB is the engine — you don't touch the engine.

Reinvestment Policy

Agree now on what percentage of profit feeds the flywheel. Suggested: 40% reinvestment / 60% distribution in Year 1 until cash reserves hit $100K. Revisit annually. Reinvestment is what separates a job from a company.

Dissolution Terms

If the business dissolves, machines sell at market rate and proceeds split by ownership percentage. Josh gets first right to repurchase any equipment to preserve his surplus-property acquisition history and federal contract past-performance record.

Culture Clause

High moral standard — no cutting corners on safety, no misleading customers, no shady contracts. Walk away from a bad deal before it taints the reputation. Any employee hired must be onboarded to the same standard. Culture is not a poster on the wall.

For Everyone in the Room

Wait — What Does Any of This Actually Mean?

If you read all of that and half the words felt like a different language, this part is for you. No jargon. No assumptions. Just the real story.

What even IS heavy equipment?
The giant machines you see at construction sites — the ones moving piles of dirt, digging holes, tearing down buildings, or clearing trees. An excavator is the big arm-and-bucket machine. A bulldozer pushes earth around with a big blade on the front. A skid steer is the smaller, nimbler one that can spin in place. These machines cost $85,000 to $500,000+ new. They are essentially very expensive, very powerful tools.
Why would he buy them cheap if he can't sell them for a year?
Because there are other ways to make money with them — ways that have nothing to do with selling or renting. Think of it like buying a commercial oven. The rule says you can't rent the oven or sell it for 18 months. But nothing stops you from opening a bakery and selling cakes. The oven is just doing what it was made to do. Same idea here.
What's the difference between renting it out and doing the work yourself?
Big legal difference. Renting = handing someone else the keys and charging them for the time. Providing a service = YOU (or someone you hired) run the machine and charge for the job completed. When a plumber comes to fix your pipes, you're not renting his pipe wrench — you're paying for the work. That's exactly what these proposals do. Your friend charges for the work, not the machine.
What does "$200 per hour" actually mean?
That's what the customer pays for one hour of the machine being operated on their job site. It covers: the machine itself, the person running it, the diesel fuel it burns, and a slice of all the other business costs. Contractors in the Southeast currently charge $170–$250/hr for excavator work. The customer has no idea what the machine cost your friend — they just know how much the hole costs them.
What is an "operator" and does he have to drive it himself?
An operator is the person who actually sits in the cab and runs the machine — like a forklift driver but for much heavier equipment. No, your friend doesn't have to do it himself. He can hire a licensed operator, pay them $28–$35/hr, charge the customer $200/hr, and keep the difference. He can run the business side — getting jobs, managing the schedule — while someone else does the physical work.
What's a "cohort" in the training proposal?
Just a group of students in one class. In Proposal 2, his machines become the classroom. Students pay tuition to learn how to operate the equipment — the same way students pay tuition to learn welding or HVAC. He's not renting the machine to them. He's selling them an education that uses the machine as the teaching tool. 8 students, 2 weeks, $6,000 each = $48,000 per session.
What does "LLC" mean and why does it matter?
LLC stands for Limited Liability Company. It's a legal structure you set up (costs $50–$500 depending on the state, takes about a week). It does one critical thing: if something goes wrong on a job — an accident, a lawsuit, a debt — the LLC is responsible, not your friend personally. His house, his savings, his truck — they're protected behind the LLC wall. For any business involving heavy machinery, this is non-negotiable. Do this on Day One.
What is Section 179 and why should I care about it?
It's an IRS tax rule that says: if you buy equipment for your business, you don't have to spread the deduction over 5–7 years like normal depreciation. You can deduct the entire purchase price the same year you buy it. So if he buys an $85,000 excavator and he's in the 35% tax bracket, the government effectively hands him back $29,750 of that purchase price in tax savings. The machine's real cost just dropped to $55,250. It's not a loophole — it was designed specifically to encourage business investment in equipment.
What is government bidding and is it really open to anyone?
Yes, literally anyone with a business license, insurance, and sometimes a bond can bid on government contracts. When a city needs a ditch dug or a county needs road repairs, they are legally required to advertise the job publicly and award it to the lowest qualified bidder. Your friend can be that bidder. Government jobs pay more per hour than private jobs (they're required to pay "prevailing wage"), they pay reliably, and they don't haggle. The catch is more paperwork and slower payment — usually 30–90 days after invoicing.
What's a "prevailing wage" and why does the government pay more?
Prevailing wage is the government's minimum labor rate for a given type of work in a given area — set by the Department of Labor. If the prevailing wage for an equipment operator in your county is $38/hr, every contractor on a government job must pay their operators at least that. This sounds like a cost — but you just build it into your bid price. The government pays for it. Your profit margin stays the same or better.
What does "ROI" mean when you see it in the tables?
Return on Investment. It's how much money you make back relative to what you put in, expressed as a percentage. A 299% ROI means for every dollar invested in the machines, you get $2.99 back in profit — in just Year One. To put that in plain terms: you spend $117,000 on equipment, and by the end of the year you've pocketed $350,100 in profit. The machines paid for themselves in about 4 months, then everything after that is money in your pocket.
What's the 15% thing about — can he really buy a $230,000 machine for $34,500?
Yes — confirmed real, though the details moved. The program is backed by Public Law 115-416 (Veterans Small Business Enhancement Act of 2018), codified at 13 CFR Part 128 Subpart G. Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Businesses get priority access to retired federal heavy equipment — apply via ADECA (Alabama's State Agency for Surplus Property) after VetCert, browse via PPMS.gov. The old portal, GSAxcess.gov, shut down in 2023; the old "flat 15-20% of replacement cost" pricing description was also wrong — it's a service charge on the government's original acquisition cost instead.
